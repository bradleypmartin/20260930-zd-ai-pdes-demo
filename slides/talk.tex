\section{Part 1: the Navier--Stokes claim}

\begin{frame}{Fluids, Newton, and a question from 1934}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{Navier--Stokes equations} are Newton's $F = ma$ for a
      fluid: water, air, blood. They sit inside aircraft design, weather
      forecasting, and ocean models.
    \item Leray's question (1934): from a smooth start, do the equations
      always predict smooth motion, or can they predict \alert{infinite speed
      in finite time} (\q{blowup})? Real fluids cannot; blowup would mean the
      model leaves its own range of validity.
    \item Clay Mathematics Institute, 2000: \$1M for an answer either way.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{2pt}
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm, y=1cm, >={Stealth[length=5pt]}, font=\footnotesize,
      box/.style={draw=inkmuted, fill=layer, rounded corners=3pt, align=center,
        inner sep=4pt, text width=3.1cm}]
    \node[box] (start) at (0, 0) {smooth start\\{\scriptsize\color{inksec} plus an optional smooth stirring force}};
    \node[box, fill=navy, draw=navy, text=surface, text width=2.4cm] (ns) at (4.6, 0) {Navier--Stokes\\equations};
    \node[box, draw=aware, text width=3.4cm] (ok) at (10.2, 0.58) {smooth motion forever\\{\scriptsize\color{inksec} the reassuring answer}};
    \node[box, draw=naive, text width=3.4cm] (bad) at (10.2, -0.58) {infinite speed at a finite time\\{\scriptsize\color{inksec} a counterexample}};
    \draw[->, inksec, thick] (start) -- (ns);
    \draw[->, aware, thick] (ns.east) -- ++(0.9, 0) |- (ok.west);

  \end{tikzpicture}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The claim, and the picture behind it}
  \begin{block}{Abstract, verbatim}
    \footnotesize\q{For every positive viscosity, we construct a solution of the
    three-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations that starts
    from rest and develops unbounded velocity in finite time while
    maintaining uniformly bounded kinetic energy.}
  \end{block}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \column{0.34\textwidth}
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.66cm, y=0.66cm, >={Stealth[length=4pt]}]
      \foreach \i/\rx/\h/\lab in {0/0.8/1.5/{early}, 1/0.45/1.35/{later}, 2/0.18/1.2/{$t \to 1$}} {
        \begin{scope}[shift={(\i*2.3, 0)}]
          \fill[aware!15, draw=aware!70] (-\rx, -\h) rectangle (\rx, \h);
          \draw[aware!70, fill=aware!25] (0, \h) ellipse [x radius=\rx, y radius=0.22*\rx];
          \draw[->, naive, thick] (\rx+0.8, 0.35) .. controls (\rx+0.45, 0.55) and (\rx+0.15, 0.45) .. (\rx+0.04, 0.15);
          \draw[->, naive, thick] (-\rx-0.8, -0.35) .. controls (-\rx-0.45, -0.55) and (-\rx-0.15, -0.45) .. (-\rx-0.04, -0.15);
          \draw[->, aware, thick] (0, 0.15) -- (0, \h+0.45);
          \draw[->, aware, thick] (0, -0.15) -- (0, -\h-0.45);
          \node[below, font=\scriptsize, inksec] at (0, -\h-0.5) {\lab};
        \end{scope}
      }
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \\[2pt]{\scriptsize\color{inkmuted} After Fig.\,1 of the manuscript. Orange: inward spiral; blue: outflow along the axis.}
    \column{0.64\textwidth}
    \small
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Starts \textbf{at rest}; a smooth stirring force drives it. Energy
        stays bounded, yet the top speed reaches infinity at $t = 1$.
      \item A spinning column gets thinner, longer, faster (\q{like
        spaghetti}). Pulling mass inward speeds the spin; viscosity loses.
      \item The force holding the bare vortex together would itself blow up;
        fine \textbf{ripples} are added whose own motion cancels the bad part.
      \item \textbf{What it is not.} Nobody has shown that turbulence on its
        own blows up. The unforced question is untouched.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \src{Source: OpenAI, \emph{Finite Time Blowup for Navier--Stokes}, Theorem 1.1 (p.\,1) and \S2, pp.\,3--6.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How it was produced, in OpenAI's own account}
  \centering
  \tile{{\Large $\sim$10,000}}{concurrent agents}\hfill
  \tile{88 h}{from launch to result}\hfill
  \tile{130 B}{output tokens}\hfill
  \tile{17 h}{more, to formalize in Lean}\hfill
  \tile{{\Large millions}}{of dollars, per Mark Chen to \emph{Science}}
  \vspace{8pt}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An internal model \q{significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra},
      in training since 28 August.
    \item Groups of \textbf{coordinating agents} with tools (a cached copy of
      the internet, code execution), each group given one of the four official
      variants of the problem.
    \item Warm-up: the unforced Euler question fell first, to \q{nearly 100
      agents} in about 50 hours. Everything was then pointed at Navier--Stokes,
      seeded with that solution.
    \item OpenAI will not claim the prize.
  \end{itemize}
  \src{Source: OpenAI's post (8 Sep, updated 10 Sep); Science, 8 Sep. Nature and Science put the warm-up at 1,000 agents; the post says \q{nearly 100}. Check press numbers against the primary source.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What \sq{machine-checked} does and does not mean}
  \begin{columns}[t]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \textbf{Lean checks}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Lean itself: a programming language for mathematics. A proof is a
        program; the compiler accepts it or rejects it.
      \item That every step follows from three standard axioms. No gaps, no
        \q{clearly}.
      \item Reproducibly: clone the repository, run the independent checker.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
    \textbf{Humans still check}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item That the formal theorem \emph{is} the Clay statement. OpenAI
        targeted an independent transcription of it (Google DeepMind's). Still
        a judgment.
      \item Whether the construction teaches anything. Clay: publication, a
        two-year wait, then a committee.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \begin{block}{Quanta, 8 Sep}
    \small The \q{crucial bit of verification that must still be done by
    humans is to guarantee that the statement being shown to be true in Lean
    is logically equivalent to what mathematicians set out to prove.}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

    \small
    \begin{itemize}

      \item A simulation stores the wave at grid points and estimates slopes
        from a few neighbours: a \textcolor{naive}{\textbf{stencil}}. One that straddles the

        that are allowed a corner there. Nothing else changes.
      \item The experiment: nine-year-old MATLAB, no licence. With Claude

\begin{frame}{2-D: scattered points and curved boundaries}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{figures/wave2d_nodes.png}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item No grid. Points are spread by a repulsion process, with fixed rows
      straddling each boundary (blue). That layout was the key to stability:
      years to find, still unproven. (2,500 shown; the runs use 10,000.)
    \item Each point's stencil is its 30 nearest neighbours, weighted with
      radial basis functions (RBF-FD). Five fields: two velocities, three
      stresses.
  \end{itemize}
